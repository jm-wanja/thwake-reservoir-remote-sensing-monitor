1Why this project exists

A large public water investment, with little independent visibility.

Thwake Dam is being built on the Athi River where Makueni and Kitui counties meet, about 180 km south-east of Nairobi. When full it will be one of Kenya's largest reservoirs, supplying drinking water, irrigation for up to ~40,000 hectares and about 20 MW of hydropower. Construction began in 2018; completion dates have slipped several times, and progress reaches the public mainly through press statements.

Satellites pass over the site every few days. With free imagery and careful methods, anyone can check how much water the reservoir actually holds — independently of official reports.

Why not use existing global tools? A 2025 comparison of five global reservoir datasets found they agree least for new reservoirs, reservoirs that change a lot, and reservoirs in developing countries — exactly Thwake's situation. A focused, carefully checked monitor fills that gap.

Three questions, answered in phases

  • How fast is it filling? — water area, level, volume and percentage full over time.
  • Is the water clean? — the main inflow, the Athi River, carries wastewater from Nairobi. Satellites can show signs of muddy water or algae (indicators, not lab tests).
  • What has the dam changed? — land flooded, water lost to evaporation, and new irrigated farmland downstream.

2Where the water will go

The shape of the reservoir, worked out before a single drop arrives.

The reservoir will stretch about 12 km up the Athi valley and into the Thwake valley. The calculated area at full level — 30.4 km² — is within 5% of the official figure (~29 km²), and sits just inside the 37 km² of land the project acquired.

A detail we found: the valley rim is only about 1 m higher than the full water level at one low point (orange dot). The 2025 design adds two small "saddle dams" to close such gaps for flood conditions; they were not yet visible on 2025 imagery. This is recorded as an open question and feeds into how uncertain the volume numbers are.

3How it works

From a satellite photo to "the reservoir is 40% full".

  1. Collect images. Two free European satellites are used together: Sentinel-2 takes ordinary colour-like photos (blocked by clouds) and Sentinel-1 uses radar, which sees through clouds and works at night — essential in the rainy seasons when filling happens.
  2. Map the water. Water looks different from land: dark in radar, and distinctive in certain colours of light. For each image an automatic threshold separates water from land, only inside the area where the reservoir can physically exist.
  3. Measure the area. Count the water pixels (each is 10 m × 10 m).
  4. Turn area into level and volume. Satellites see how big the lake is, not how deep. But because we know the valley's shape from before the dam, we know exactly how much water corresponds to each lake size — like knowing a bowl's shape and reading the volume from how wide the water surface is.
  5. Report with honesty. Every published number carries a range (not a single figure) and the date of the image it came from.

The difference between the two models mostly comes from a small height offset between them, not from a different valley shape — and for the same measured lake area they agree within about 1%, which is what matters when converting satellite areas into volumes.

What "frozen" means here: the baseline files were approved on 10 October 2026, tagged baseline-v1, and fingerprinted. An automatic check fails if any of them changes. Changing them requires a new, documented version.

5Proving it works — on a reservoir that already exists

Before trusting the method on Thwake, it is tested where real measurements exist.

Thwake has no water yet, so there is nothing to check against. The method is therefore first tested on Masinga Reservoir on the Tana River, Kenya's largest, operated by KenGen since 1981. Satellite-measured lake areas are converted into water levels and compared with:

  • KenGen's published gauge levels (from press statements), and
  • DAHITI, an independent satellite water-level record from the Technical University of Munich (96 readings, 2018–2026). It is satellite-based too, so it is an independent check rather than ground truth.

Useful problems found early: radar can mistake wet, exposed lake bed for water when levels are low. Across 2019–2026, whenever the lake was low, radar measured on average ~26 km² more water than optical images — exactly what Thwake will look like when it starts filling. One optical image also failed silently. Both are being turned into automatic checks before the Thwake tracker goes live.

Rapid changes matter too: in late 2023 Masinga rose about 9 m in under a month, so comparisons made a few days apart during such rises are flagged rather than trusted.

Next in validation: hand-drawn shorelines for about 25 images to score the water maps directly, an automatic accuracy test that runs on every change, and an optional comparison with a machine-learning classifier.

6How the system is built

Free tools end to end, and designed to update itself.

The heavy image processing happens inside Google Earth Engine (free for non-commercial research), so only small summary files are downloaded and stored. Everything is written in Python, configured in plain text files rather than hard-coded, and can be re-run from scratch by anyone using public data.

7Roadmap

One phase at a time, each with a clear "done" test.

After that, the monitor updates itself monthly, with a human review and short public update after each rainy season. Later, the same pipeline could cover other Kenyan reservoirs — starting with Masinga, which is already set up for validation.

8Quality and trust

How the project tries to deserve being believed.

Checked automatically

  • Over 240 automated tests run on every change, plus style and type checks
  • The frozen baseline is fingerprinted; any change fails the checks
  • Scans block passwords, keys and oversized files from ever being published
  • Dependencies are pinned and updated through reviewed requests

Checked by people

  • Every fact cites a source; unverified facts are marked and listed
  • Key figures (full level, capacity, dam position) were verified by the author against official documents and high-resolution imagery
  • Every important choice is written down with the alternatives considered (11 decision records so far)
  • Each piece of work is reviewed before it is merged
How AI was used: AI coding agents drafted much of the code and documentation, one clearly defined task at a time, under written rules. They can read the project's history but are technically blocked from changing it: the author reviews every change, makes every decision, and does every commit and publication.

9What this project cannot claim

Being clear about limits is part of the method.

  • Volumes are estimates with a range. Elevation models are accurate to a few metres in height; at Thwake, 1 m of water level is roughly 30 million m³.
  • Water-quality results are indicators, not lab tests. The project will say "higher muddiness signal", never "safe" or "contaminated".
  • No official operating data. Everything is inferred from imagery unless official figures are published.
  • Clouds and timing. Optical images are often blocked in the rains; radar fills gaps but has its own errors at low water levels.
  • Land-cover maps disagree. Two global maps give very different figures for the land that will be flooded, so only ranges are reported.
  • Reservoirs silt up. Masinga lost an estimated ~14% of its storage to sediment in 30 years. The frozen "before" shape will slowly overstate Thwake's volume over decades.
  • Filling dates depend on rain and construction. If filling is slow or delayed, that is reported as a result in itself.

10A few terms

Glossary
Remote sensing
Measuring things from a distance — here, from satellites.
Sentinel-1 / Sentinel-2
Free European Space Agency satellites. Sentinel-1 uses radar (sees through clouds); Sentinel-2 takes optical images, 10 m detail.
Elevation model
A map of ground height. Ones made before the dam show the empty valley's shape.
Full supply level
The normal maximum water level: 912 m above sea level at Thwake.
Million m³ (MCM)
One million cubic metres = one billion litres.
Baseline
The "before" measurements that all later results are compared with.
Validation
Checking a method against independent measurements to show how accurate it is.
Altimetry
Satellites measuring water height by timing a radar pulse to the surface and back.
Google Earth Engine
Google's cloud platform for analysing satellite data, free for non-commercial research.
